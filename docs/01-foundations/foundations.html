Foundations: math + quantum concepts

The starter → intermediate deep dive for Phase 1 of the ramp. Enough linear algebra and quantum intuition to read a state vector, predict a measurement, and understand what every later Q# example is actually doing. How to use this

Work top to bottom; each section assumes the one before it. Math renders with MathJax (needs an internet connection the first time you open the page).

Prerequisites: comfort with high-school algebra and functions. No prior physics or linear algebra assumed — we build the linear algebra you need here. If you already know complex numbers, unitaries, and the tensor product, skim §1–§3 and start at §4.

Notation: $i=\sqrt{-1}$; $z^\ast$ is the complex conjugate; vectors are columns; $A^\dagger$ is the conjugate transpose; $|\psi\rangle$ is a quantum state (a unit vector).

01 Complex numbers starter

Goal: read and manipulate $a+bi$, compute a modulus and a phase, and see why phase is the currency of quantum computing.

A complex number is $z = a + bi$ with real part $a$ and imaginary part $b$, where $i^2=-1$. Its complex conjugate is $z^\ast = a - bi$. The modulus (length) is $$|z| = \sqrt{z^\ast z} = \sqrt{a^2+b^2},$$ and the phase (angle) is $\varphi = \operatorname{atan2}(b,a)$, so that $z = |z|\,e^{i\varphi}$ by Euler's formula $e^{i\varphi} = \cos\varphi + i\sin\varphi$.

Why you care: a qubit's amplitudes are complex numbers. The probability of an outcome is the modulus-squared of an amplitude, $|z|^2 = a^2+b^2$, so the phase $\varphi$ never shows up in a single measurement — but it controls how amplitudes interfere when gates combine them. Interference is where all quantum advantage comes from.

Worked example

Let $z = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1 + i)$. Then $|z|^2 = \tfrac12(1^2+1^2) = 1$, so $|z|=1$: this is a pure phase. Its angle is $\varphi = \operatorname{atan2}(1,1) = 45^\circ = \pi/4$, i.e. $z = e^{i\pi/4}$.

Check yourself: is $e^{i\pi} + 1$ really $0$?
Yes. $e^{i\pi} = \cos\pi + i\sin\pi = -1 + 0i = -1$, so $e^{i\pi}+1 = 0$. A global phase of $-1$ multiplies the whole state and is physically invisible; a relative phase of $-1$ between two amplitudes (as $Z$ produces) is very visible.
Connect to Q#: the S and T gates apply phases $e^{i\pi/2}$ and $e^{i\pi/4}$; Rz(θ, q) applies a $z$-axis phase rotation. You'll use these in §5.

Exercises

  • Compute $|{\tfrac{3}{5} + \tfrac{4}{5}i}|$ and its phase.
  • Write $-i$ in the form $e^{i\varphi}$.
  • Show that multiplying an amplitude by $e^{i\varphi}$ leaves $|z|^2$ unchanged.

02 Vectors, matrices & unitaries starter → intermediate

Goal: represent states as column vectors, transform them with matrices, and recognise the unitary matrices that quantum gates must be.

A single qubit lives in a 2-dimensional complex vector space $\mathbb{C}^2$. States are column vectors $$|\psi\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\ \beta\end{pmatrix},\qquad \alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}.$$ The inner product of $|\psi\rangle$ and $|\phi\rangle$ is $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle = \phi_0^\ast\alpha + \phi_1^\ast\beta$, and the norm is $\||\psi\rangle\| = \sqrt{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}$. Quantum states are always unit vectors: $|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2 = 1$.

Gates are matrices $U$ acting by $|\psi\rangle \mapsto U|\psi\rangle$. Because a gate must preserve total probability (keep unit vectors as unit vectors), $U$ must be unitary: $$U^\dagger U = U U^\dagger = I,$$ where $U^\dagger$ is the conjugate transpose. Unitary $\Rightarrow$ invertible, and $U^{-1}=U^\dagger$. This is exactly why Q# can auto-generate the Adjoint of an operation: the inverse is just the dagger.

Worked example

The Hadamard matrix $H = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\ 1&-1\end{pmatrix}$ applied to $|0\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}$ gives $H|0\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\ 1\end{pmatrix}$. Check unitarity: $H^\dagger = H$ and $H^2 = I$, so $H$ is its own inverse.

Check yourself: why can't a gate be the matrix $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$?
It isn't unitary — it maps $|1\rangle$ to the zero vector, destroying the norm, and it has no inverse. Physically it would be an irreversible "erase," which unitary evolution forbids. Measurement, not a gate, is the irreversible step.
Connect to Q#: an operation ... : Unit is Adj + Ctl promises the compiler can build its adjoint (inverse) and a controlled version — possible precisely because gates are unitary.

Exercises

  • Verify $\langle 0|1\rangle = 0$ and $\langle 0|0\rangle = 1$ for the computational basis.
  • Show the Pauli $X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ is unitary and equals its own inverse.
  • Compute $H\,|1\rangle$.

03 Dirac (bra–ket) notation starter

Goal: fluently read $|\psi\rangle$, $\langle\psi|$, $\langle a|b\rangle$, and $|a\rangle\langle b|$ — the shorthand every quantum text and the Q# docs use.

A ket $|\psi\rangle$ is a column vector (a state). A bra $\langle\psi|$ is its conjugate transpose, a row vector. Putting a bra next to a ket forms the inner product (a number), $\langle a|b\rangle$; putting a ket next to a bra forms an outer product (a matrix), $|a\rangle\langle b|$. The computational basis is $$|0\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad |1\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}.$$ Any qubit is $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$.

Worked example

$\langle 0|1\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix} = 0$ — the basis states are orthogonal. And $|0\rangle\langle 0| = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ is the projector onto $|0\rangle$; measurement probabilities are built from projectors like this.

Check yourself: what is $\langle +|+\rangle$ where $|+\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)$?
$1$. Expand: $\tfrac12(\langle0|+\langle1|)(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)=\tfrac12(1+0+0+1)=1$. Every valid state is normalised to length 1.

Exercises

  • Write $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)$ as column vectors and show $\langle+|-\rangle = 0$.
  • Compute the matrix $|0\rangle\langle 1|$.

04 The qubit & the Bloch sphere intermediate

Goal: picture a single-qubit state as a point on a sphere, and connect the angles $\theta,\varphi$ to amplitudes.

Up to a physically-irrelevant global phase, any single-qubit state can be written with two real angles: $$|\psi\rangle = \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,|0\rangle + e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,|1\rangle,$$ with polar angle $\theta\in[0,\pi]$ and azimuth $\varphi\in[0,2\pi)$. Map these to a point on the unit sphere: $|0\rangle$ is the north pole, $|1\rangle$ the south pole, and the equator holds the equal-superposition states $|+\rangle,|-\rangle,|{+}i\rangle,|{-}i\rangle$.

Reading the picture: the polar angle sets the $|0\rangle$ vs $|1\rangle$ measurement probabilities ($P(0)=\cos^2\tfrac{\theta}{2}$), while the azimuthal angle $\varphi$ is the relative phase — invisible to a $Z$-basis measurement but rotatable by $Z$-axis gates and visible if you measure in the $X$ basis.

Check yourself: where is $|+\rangle$ on the sphere?
On the equator, pointing along $+x$: $\theta=\tfrac{\pi}{2}$, $\varphi=0$, giving $\cos\tfrac{\pi}{4}|0\rangle + \sin\tfrac{\pi}{4}|1\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)$.
Connect to Q#: DumpMachine() prints amplitudes; a single qubit after H(q) shows $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$ on both $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ — the $|+\rangle$ point above. See examples/01_hello_qubit.qs.

Exercises

  • What $(\theta,\varphi)$ give $|{-}\rangle$? And $|{+}i\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+i|1\rangle)$?
  • If $P(0)=\tfrac34$, what is $\theta$?

05 Single-qubit gates intermediate

Goal: know the standard one-qubit gates as matrices and as rotations, and predict their effect on basis states.
GateMatrixEffect
$X$$\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$bit flip: $|0\rangle\leftrightarrow|1\rangle$
$Z$$\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$phase flip on $|1\rangle$
$Y$$\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}$bit + phase flip
$H$$\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$basis $\leftrightarrow$ superposition
$S,T$$\operatorname{diag}(1,e^{i\pi/2}),\ \operatorname{diag}(1,e^{i\pi/4})$add relative phase

The Paulis $X,Y,Z$ generate rotations about the Bloch axes: $R_x(\theta)=e^{-i\theta X/2}$, and similarly $R_y,R_z$. A useful fact you'll lean on: $H$ turns a $Z$-basis state into an $X$-basis state, which is why a "measure in the $X$ basis" is often coded as $H$ then a $Z$-measurement.

Worked example

$H|0\rangle = |+\rangle$, then $Z|+\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)=|-\rangle$, then $H|-\rangle = |1\rangle$. So $HZH = X$: a phase flip conjugated by Hadamards is a bit flip. This identity is the seed of many circuit simplifications.

Check yourself: what does $T$ do to $|0\rangle$? To $|1\rangle$?
Nothing to $|0\rangle$ (the top-left entry is 1). It multiplies $|1\rangle$ by $e^{i\pi/4}$, a relative phase. On $|+\rangle$ it produces $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+e^{i\pi/4}|1\rangle)$, rotating the Bloch point $45^\circ$ around the equator.
Connect to Q#: these are intrinsic operations — X(q), H(q), Rz(theta, q). Play with the biased coin in examples/02_coin_flip.qs (it uses Ry).

Exercises

  • Show $X = HZH$ by multiplying the matrices.
  • What single rotation $R_y(\theta)$ prepares a state with $P(1)=0.3$? (Hint: $P(1)=\sin^2(\theta/2)$.)
  • Verify $S^2 = Z$ and $T^2 = S$.

06 Superposition & measurement intermediate

Goal: apply the Born rule, understand collapse, and see how the measurement basis changes the outcome.

For $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$, a computational-basis ($Z$) measurement returns $0$ with probability $|\alpha|^2$ and $1$ with probability $|\beta|^2$ (the Born rule). Measurement is irreversible: afterwards the state collapses to the observed basis state. Only $|\alpha|^2$ and $|\beta|^2$ matter for one measurement — the relative phase is invisible in this basis but reappears if you measure in a different basis.

Worked example

$|+\rangle$ measured in $Z$: $P(0)=P(1)=\tfrac12$ — a fair coin. The same $|+\rangle$ measured in the $X$ basis is deterministic: it is the $+1$ eigenstate of $X$, so you always get the "$+$" outcome. Different question (basis) $\Rightarrow$ different certainty.

Check yourself: measure $Z|+\rangle=|-\rangle$ in the $Z$ basis — what do you see?
Still $50/50$. $|-\rangle$ has the same magnitudes as $|+\rangle$; the phase flip is invisible to a $Z$-measurement. Measure in $X$ and you'd get the deterministic "$-$" outcome instead — that's how the phase becomes observable.
Connect to Q#: run examples/05_measurement_bases.qs — it measures the same $|+\rangle$ in $X$ (deterministic) and $Z$ (random) so you can watch the difference.

Exercises

  • For $|\psi\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}|0\rangle+\sqrt{\tfrac23}\,|1\rangle$, give $P(0)$ and $P(1)$.
  • Why does a global phase $e^{i\gamma}|\psi\rangle$ produce identical measurement statistics?

07 Tensor products & multi-qubit states intermediate

Goal: combine qubits with the tensor product, and see why $n$ qubits need $2^n$ amplitudes.

Two qubits combine by the tensor (Kronecker) product: $|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$, written $|ab\rangle$. For basis states, $$|0\rangle\otimes|0\rangle=|00\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\\0\end{pmatrix},\quad |1\rangle\otimes|0\rangle=|10\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}.$$ A general two-qubit state has four amplitudes; $n$ qubits have $2^n$. This exponential growth is the resource quantum computing exploits — and the reason classical simulation gets hard fast.

Worked example

Apply $H$ to the first of two qubits in $|00\rangle$: $H|0\rangle\otimes|0\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)\otimes|0\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+|10\rangle)$. This is still a product state — the two qubits are independent.

Check yourself: is $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)$ a product state?
No. If it were $(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle)\otimes(\gamma|0\rangle+\delta|1\rangle)$ you'd need $\alpha\delta=0$ and $\beta\gamma=0$ (no $|01\rangle,|10\rangle$ terms) yet $\alpha\gamma,\beta\delta\ne0$ — impossible. It can't be factored: that's entanglement (§8).
Connect to Q#: use qs = Qubit[n] allocates an $n$-qubit register; see the GHZ register in examples/04_ghz_state.qs.

Exercises

  • Write $|01\rangle$ as a 4-vector and as $|0\rangle\otimes|1\rangle$.
  • How many complex amplitudes describe 10 qubits?

08 Entanglement & no-cloning intermediate

Goal: define entanglement via Bell states, and understand the no-cloning theorem and why it shapes quantum programming.

An entangled state cannot be written as a tensor product of single-qubit states. The canonical example is the Bell state $$|\Phi^+\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle),$$ built by $H$ on the first qubit followed by $\mathrm{CNOT}$. Measuring either qubit yields $0$ or $1$ at random, but the two results are perfectly correlated: see one, and you know the other, no matter how far apart. This correlation — not faster-than-light signalling — is the resource behind teleportation and superdense coding.

The no-cloning theorem: there is no unitary that copies an arbitrary unknown state, $|\psi\rangle|0\rangle \mapsto |\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle$ for all $|\psi\rangle$. A quick argument: cloning would have to be linear (unitaries are), but copying is inherently nonlinear in the amplitudes, so the two requirements conflict. Consequences you'll feel in Q#: you can't "save a backup" of a qubit, debugging can't duplicate mid-circuit states, and error correction must spread information across many qubits rather than copying it.

Worked example
// Bell state |Φ+⟩ — the smallest entangled system
use (a, b) = (Qubit(), Qubit());
H(a);          // (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 on a
CNOT(a, b);    // entangle: now (|00⟩+|11⟩)/√2
// measuring a and b always agrees
Check yourself: does entanglement let you send a message instantly?
No. Each local measurement looks perfectly random; the correlation only shows up when the two parties later compare results over a classical channel. This is the "no-signalling" principle — entanglement is a correlation resource, not a telegraph.
Connect to Q#: build and measure a Bell pair in examples/03_bell_state.qs; extend it to a register in 04_ghz_state.qs. These set up Phase 5's teleportation.

Exercises

  • Write the other three Bell states and the circuits that make them (hint: precede with $X$ and/or $Z$).
  • Explain in one sentence why no-cloning does not forbid copying a known classical bit.
